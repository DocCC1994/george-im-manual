Definitions (Sepsis-3) (STG 2024, 23.10)

  • Sepsis: life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Organ dysfunction = an acute rise in SOFA score of ≥ 2 points. "SIRS" and "severe sepsis" are no longer used as definitions.
  • Septic shock: sepsis with hypotension needing a vasopressor to keep MAP ≥ 65 mmHg despite adequate fluid, plus lactate > 2 mmol/L. Mortality > 20% [STG] CHECK figure
  • Sepsis is a clinical diagnosis; no single test rules it in or out [SSC 2026, GPS].

Screening (SSC 2026, recs 1–6)

  • Use NEWS2 (or MEWS or SIRS) rather than qSOFA as the single screening tool (strong, moderate). qSOFA (RR ≥ 22, altered mentation, SBP ≤ 100) predicts death but misses many early cases.
  • Hospitals should run a sepsis improvement programme with screening and a standard procedure (strong), and may use a "code sepsis" team call (conditional).
  • Lactate is suggested in every suspected case; serial lactate and capillary refill time can guide resuscitation (conditional).

Why the first hour matters

In septic shock, each hour of delay in effective antibiotics adds about 7.8% mortality [SAASP, citing Kumar 2006]. SSC 2026 keeps the 1-hour target for shock and probable sepsis, but for possible sepsis without shock allows up to 3 hours to look for another cause, to avoid giving broad-spectrum antibiotics to patients who are not infected (recs 16–20).

The South African picture
  • HIV: think of TB (disseminated TB can look like Gram-negative sepsis), PCP, cryptococcus, non-typhoidal Salmonella. Send an HIV test in every case CHECK
  • Malaria after travel to a malaria area: test before calling it bacterial sepsis.
  • Resistance: in hospital-acquired infection, ESBL Klebsiella is common. At Groote Schuur (2015) only 48% of ESBL K. pneumoniae were sensitive to anti-pseudomonal BL/BLI (piperacillin-tazobactam), but 95% to aminoglycoside (amikacin) [SAASP Ch 19]. George has no local antibiogram and follows SAASP.
  • Blood cultures: two sets is optimal; one is often taken because of cost. Volume matters most after timing: 10 mL per adult bottle [SAASP Ch 3].

Mimics

PE, bleeding, cardiogenic shock, adrenal crisis, DKA, pancreatitis, poisoning (salicylate, organophosphate), anaphylaxis, thyroid storm, drug reaction (DRESS), heat stroke. Stop empiric antibiotics if another cause is proven [SSC, GPS].

Behind the recommendations (SSC 2026)

  • Vasopressor choice. SSC keeps noradrenaline over adrenaline as first-line (carried over from 2021; low-certainty evidence for this comparison). With cardiac dysfunction either is acceptable: noradrenaline if tachycardic or in a tachyarrhythmia, adrenaline if bradycardic. The STG chooses adrenaline because it is on the EML, cheap and widely stocked.
  • Fluid volume. Four trials (3 320 patients, including CLOVERS) of restrictive against liberal fluid after the first 30 mL/kg found no mortality difference, so SSC leaves later volume to judgement and to whether a monitored bed is available. Fluid overload worsens ARDS, which is why the STG warns against over-hydrating.
  • Balanced fluid. In a meta-analysis led by SMART, BaSICS and PLUS, balanced crystalloid probably reduced mortality slightly (OR 0.94, CI 0.85–1.04) and new dialysis (OR 0.86) compared with saline.
  • Steroids speed shock reversal and may slightly reduce mortality, at the cost of hyperglycaemia and hypernatraemia. No benefit above about 260 mg/day of hydrocortisone.
  • Prolonged β-lactam infusion keeps the level above the MIC for longer; a meta-analysis of 18 trials including BLING III showed lower short-term mortality (RR 0.91; about 25 fewer deaths per 1 000). It needs a dedicated line and pump.
Not recommended (SSC 2026)
  • Starches or gelatin
  • Dopamine, terlipressin
  • Vitamin C, vitamin D, IV immunoglobulin
  • Blood purification, polymyxin B haemoperfusion
  • Antipyretics to improve outcome (comfort is fine)
  • β-blockers to treat septic shock
  • Sodium bicarbonate for lactic acidosis, unless pH ≤ 7.2 with AKI
  • Routine empiric antifungals
After sepsis

Survivors often have weakness, cognitive and mood problems, and readmission. Reconcile medicines, screen for new problems and arrange follow-up at discharge [SSC].
