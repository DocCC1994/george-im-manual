Overview and definitions

  • Severe malaria: P. falciparum asexual parasitaemia + ≥ 1 clinical or laboratory criterion with no other cause (table below) [NDoH 2019, WHO]. Severe vivax or knowlesi: same criteria, no parasite threshold. Case fatality 10–40% despite treatment.
  • Hyperparasitaemia: > 4% of red cells (or 3+) in the non-immune; schizonts on the film are a marker of severity. 2–4% carries a higher risk of treatment failure. A low count does not exclude severe disease (sequestered parasites are not seen) [NDoH 7.5.11].
  • South Africa: almost everyone is non-immune, including residents of the seasonal transmission areas (Limpopo, Mpumalanga, northern KwaZulu-Natal). Most Western Cape cases are imported (Mozambique, Zimbabwe, Malawi, central Africa) CHECK. Odyssean (airport, taxi, suitcase) malaria is often diagnosed late: case fatality 11% in 2014–2016 [NDoH]. Category 1 notifiable: within 24 h.
  • Higher risk: pregnancy (hypoglycaemia, ARDS), HIV (1.7–2.7-fold more severe malaria in adults, more AKI and bacteraemia), age > 65, asplenia [NDoH 8.1, 8.3].

Pathophysiology

  • Sequestration: infected red cells stick to venular endothelium → microvascular obstruction → tissue hypoxia, lactic acidosis (the strongest predictor of death), coma. Fluid does not open blocked capillaries, which is why boluses do not help [NDoH 7.4].
  • Lungs: raised permeability → pulmonary oedema and ARDS, often days after starting treatment; worst in pregnancy. Kidneys: sequestration, haemolysis and haemoglobinuria → ATN; usually reversible.
  • Blood: haemolysis and marrow suppression → anaemia; platelets low in most cases (bleeding is uncommon). DIC with multi-organ failure or bacteraemia. Glucose: hypoglycaemia from the parasite, quinine (insulin release) and pregnancy.

Stepwise work-up

  • Step 1: thick and thin smear with % parasitaemia and species; RDT (HRP2) in parallel. Glucose, FBC, U&E, ALT, bilirubin, gas with lactate, blood cultures, HIV, β-hCG.
  • Step 2: CXR (breathless, SpO2 < 92%); LP when consciousness is impaired and meningitis is possible; CT brain only if focal signs.
  • Step 3, pitfalls: RDT stays positive for weeks (not for follow-up); parasitaemia may rise in the first 24 h; gametocytes persist up to 2 weeks and are not failure; mixed-species RDT bands are often misread. PCR only for repeatedly negative or discordant tests, not to follow response [NDoH Annex 3].
Antimalarial pearls
  • Artesunate beats quinine: lower mortality in adults [SEAQUAMAT 2005] and children [AQUAMAT 2010] CHECK trial figures; faster clearance, no hypoglycaemia, no QT effect.
  • Post-artesunate delayed haemolysis from about day 7 in hyperparasitaemic non-immune patients: follow up Hb [NDoH 7.3.2].
  • Quinine: dose as salt (10 mg salt = 8.3 mg base). Load by actual weight; maintenance by ideal weight if obese (IBW men 0.9 × cm − 88; women 0.9 × cm − 92). After 48 h with AKI or no improvement: 10 mg/kg 12hrly. Not dialysed. Deaths in SA came from no load, fast infusion, repeated loads and missed hypoglycaemia [NDoH]. Cinchonism (tinnitus, deafness) is expected, not a reason to stop.
  • Oral step-down: complete a full 6-dose course of ACT (artemether-lumefantrine); quinine + tetracycline (doxycycline) or, in pregnancy, lincosamide (clindamycin) for 7 days if ACT cannot be used.
  • P. ovale or vivax: primaquine (Section 21) for radical cure after G6PD testing; not in pregnancy.
Fluid in adults (NDoH Fig 3)
  • MAP > 65, urine > 0.5 mL/kg per hour: maintenance 1–2 mL/kg per hour only.
  • Oliguria: 5 mL/kg trial over 15 min; no response → RRT.
  • Anuria: RRT, no fluid challenge.
  • Pulmonary oedema: no IV fluid; oxygen, CPAP, diuretic.
  • MAP < 65 with poor perfusion: cautious bolus, vasopressor, look for sepsis or bleeding.
  • No colloids.
Not recommended

Exchange transfusion (no proven benefit [STG]) · corticosteroids, mannitol, heparin in cerebral malaria · prophylactic anticonvulsants · NSAIDs and aspirin · bicarbonate for acidosis [NDoH]

Tick bite fever (STG §9.10); (Taljaard)

  • Two diseases: African tick bite fever (R. africae, Amblyomma ticks on cattle and game; rural, clusters; multiple eschars, painful regional nodes; rash in about half, may be vesicular; mild) and Mediterranean spotted fever (R. conorii, brown dog tick; single eschar, rash in > 95% including palms and soles; can be severe). Spring and summer peak.
  • Mechanism: intracellular infection of endothelium → focal vasculitis in many organs: capillary leak, rash, encephalitis, pneumonitis, myocarditis, AKI, hepatitis, VTE. Whole-organ failure is uncommon; severe disease usually follows late treatment.
  • Work-up pitfalls: no bedside test; serology turns positive only in week 2; Weil-Felix is neither sensitive nor specific; a good response to doxycycline within 48 h confirms the diagnosis.
  • Treatment: doxycycline 100 mg 12hrly for 7 days (3–5 days may suffice), no renal adjustment. IV tetracyclines are not available in SA: FQ (ciprofloxacin) 400 mg IV 8hrly (inferior) or chloramphenicol 500 mg IV 6hrly have been used CHECK chloramphenicol stock. Pregnancy or mild disease: macrolide (azithromycin), but in severe disease doxycycline for 24–48 h first.

Viral haemorrhagic fever (NDoH 2014); (STG §9.5)

  • Endemic in SA: Crimean-Congo (Hyalomma "bontpoot" tick, livestock; mortality about 30%, deaths day 5–14; spreads by blood and body fluids, including nosocomial outbreaks) and Rift Valley fever (livestock, mosquitoes; < 1% haemorrhagic). Imported: Ebola, Marburg, Lassa (incubation up to 3 weeks); Lujo (Johannesburg, 2008: 4 of 5 died, 3 infected before isolation).
  • Congo fever score (Swanepoel 1987): exposure (tick bite, livestock blood, patient contact: 2–3 points), symptoms (sudden onset, fever, headache, myalgia, vomiting: 1 each; bleeding: 3) and day 1–5 labs (WCC, platelets, INR, PTT, AST/ALT: 1–2 each). ≥ 12 points: treat as CCHF.
  • Risk category (decides isolation and transfer): low, features of VHF but no exposure and urban for 3 weeks; moderate, rural, animal or tick exposure; high, fever with bleeding, or a definite exposure (health worker, contact).
  • Poor prognosis in CCHF (days 1–5): WCC ≥ 10, platelets ≤ 20, AST ≥ 200, ALT ≥ 150, APTT ≥ 60 s, fibrinogen ≤ 1.1 g/L. Ribavirin (benefit unproven; severe early CCHF, Lassa): oral 2 g load, 1 g 6hrly to day 4, 500 mg 6hrly to day 10 CHECK DECISION

Escalation and red flags
  • Call GRH IM for every severe case; ICU (GRH ICU SOP) for coma, shock, ARDS, lactate > 5 or dialysis.
  • Suspected VHF: consultant, infection control, management now; NICD before any specimen; transfer only once agreed.
  • Never: quinine as a bolus or repeat load; boluses with crackles; a β-lactam alone for rickettsiosis.
