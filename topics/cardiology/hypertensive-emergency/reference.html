Terms and thresholds

TermMeaning
Severe hypertensionSBP > 180 and/or DBP > 110 [STG 2024 §3.6]; ESC 2024 uses ≥ 180/110 as the level at which an emergency must be excluded I C [ESC 2024]. AHA/ACC 2025 and GRH 2021 use > 180/120 [AHA/ACC 2025].
Hypertensive emergencySevere hypertension with acute, ongoing organ damage (brain, retina, heart, aorta, kidney, blood vessels, placenta). Needs immediate, controlled lowering, usually IV, in high care [STG §3.6.3].
Malignant hypertensionAn emergency with severe BP (often > 200/120) and advanced retinopathy (flame haemorrhages, cotton-wool spots, papilloedema), often with AKI and TMA. “Acute hypertensive microangiopathy” describes it better [ESC Council 2019].
Hypertensive encephalopathySevere hypertension with lethargy, seizures, cortical blindness or coma and no other cause; MRI often shows posterior reversible encephalopathy (PRES).
TMACoombs-negative haemolysis with fragments and low platelets that improves as BP is controlled. Exclude TTP (very low platelets, ADAMTS13) and HIV-associated TMA.
“Urgency”STG keeps a middle tier: symptomatic or progressing organ damage without a life-threatening complication, treated orally in hospital (§3.6.2). ESC and AHA/ACC drop the word: severe hypertension without acute organ damage is treated orally, usually as an outpatient DECISION: terms used at George

Why not lower it quickly?

Cerebral, coronary and renal blood flow are autoregulated over a range of pressures. In chronic hypertension that range shifts upward, so a pressure that is normal for someone else can cause ischaemia. Most guidance therefore lowers BP by about a quarter over the first hours and to normal over 24–48 h. The exceptions are those where minutes matter: aortic dissection, pulmonary oedema, ACS and eclampsia. There are no randomised trials comparing rates of lowering; the targets are expert consensus [ESC Council 2019].

Tertiary options · work-up

1. Renal biopsy for unexplained AKI with proteinuria once BP is controlled.

2. Renal artery imaging (CT or MR angiography) and revascularisation in selected patients.

3. Aldosterone/renin ratio, metanephrines and adrenal imaging if not available locally.

4. ADAMTS13 and plasma exchange if TTP cannot be excluded.

Special situations

Aortic dissection. Shear stress depends on heart rate and the rate of rise of pressure, so the BB goes first, then a vasodilator: SBP < 120 and HR < 60 within 20 minutes [ESC 2024 aorta] [ESC Council]. If BBs are contraindicated, a rate-limiting (non-dihydropyridine) CCB is the alternative CHECK. IV opioid (morphine) analgesia reduces the sympathetic drive. Type A (ascending aorta) goes to surgery; uncomplicated type B is treated medically.

Ischaemic stroke and ICH. All sources agree for thrombolysis (< 185/110 before, < 180/105 for 24 h) and that BP is left alone below 220/120 if no thrombolysis is planned (STG, GRH 2021, ESC). They differ for ICH with SBP 150–220: STG and GRH 2021 say do not lower (GRH quoted harm from a target < 140); ESC 2024 aims for 140–160 within the first hours and forbids a fall of > 70 mmHg in the first hour when SBP ≥ 220 (III B) [ESC 2024] DECISION: agree with the stroke topic

Stimulants (tik, cocaine). Benzodiazepine (diazepam) treats the agitation, tachycardia and hypertension. Then GTN; alternatives: CCB, IV α-blocker. ESC Council advises against BBs, labetalol included, because of unopposed alpha stimulation and coronary spasm; the human evidence for harm is weak DECISION

Phaeochromocytoma crisis. α-blockade first (IV α-blocker, then an oral α-blocker) via endocrinology; a BB only after α-blockade. Labetalol has caused paradoxical hypertension.

Pregnancy. ≥ 160/110 is an emergency (ESC 2024 lists ≥ 170/110 CHECK); admit, MgSO4 for severe pre-eclampsia, and follow the obstetric protocol. ESC 2024 recommends an IV α/β-blocker, oral central α-agonist or oral CCB I C, with an IV arteriolar vasodilator second-line CHECK SA maternity guideline thresholds and doses

Scleroderma renal crisis. ACEi (captopril, increased daily) is the treatment, even with AKI; refer to rheumatology and nephrology.

From IV to oral

  • Start oral drugs once the BP has been controlled and stable for 8–24 h; overlap, then wean the IV drug (GRH 2021).
  • Malignant hypertension and TMA are renin-driven. ACEi (enalapril) helps, but the first dose can drop the BP sharply in a volume-depleted patient: 2.5 mg test dose, then increase to 20 mg daily, with K+ and creatinine at 24–48 h [STG §3.6.3]. Avoid if Na+ < 130 (marker of high renin). Alternative if the ACEi is not tolerated: ARB (STG: specialist-initiated).
  • Then amlodipine and thiazide (HCTZ); loop diuretic if eGFR < 30. Most patients need three drugs; MRA (spironolactone) is the usual fourth.
  • Creatinine often rises in the first days of BP control in malignant hypertension. If the patient is not overloaded and K+ is safe, continue; kidney function frequently recovers over weeks.

Follow-up and prognosis

  • See monthly at least until at target and organ damage (creatinine, proteinuria, LVH) is regressing [ESC Council 2019]. Target thereafter < 140/90 (STG) or 120–129 systolic if tolerated (ESC 2024) DECISION: clinic target
  • Simplify the regimen: once-daily drugs, fixed-dose combinations where available, the same drugs as the clinic stocks.
  • Untreated malignant hypertension was fatal within a year in most patients; with treatment, most now survive 5 years, but dialysis dependence remains common when creatinine is high at presentation CHECK figures for a SA cohort
  • Screen for secondary causes (page 1) in every patient under 40, with low K+, or with resistant hypertension.
Discharge checklist
  1. Cause and organ damage documented (fundi, creatinine, urine PCR, ECG)
  2. Stable on oral drugs for 24 h
  3. Bloods 1 week after an ACEi or diuretic was started
  4. Clinic date within 1–2 weeks; letter with drugs and targets
  5. Stimulant or alcohol use addressed
  6. Contraception discussed if on an ACEi or ARB
