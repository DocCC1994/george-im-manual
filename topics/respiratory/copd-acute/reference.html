Definition and causes (GOLD 2026, ch 4; STG 2024 §16.4)

An acute event with worse dyspnoea and/or cough and sputum over up to 14 days, often with tachypnoea or tachycardia, from airway inflammation triggered by infection or pollution. Most are viral (rhinovirus, influenza, RSV); bacteria (S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, M. catarrhalis) and smoke from biomass fuel or fires also trigger them. STG still uses the older definition (severe = needed an oral steroid or antibiotic); GOLD now grades severity at the bedside (Rome proposal) rather than after the event.

About 15–30% of admitted patients have a mild event by Rome criteria, 50% moderate and 10% severe. Five-year mortality after an admission is about 50%; 30-day readmission 30–50% [GOLD 2026].

Why controlled oxygen

Hypercapnia from high FiO2 is mostly loss of hypoxic vasoconstriction (worse V/Q mismatch) and the Haldane effect, not only "loss of hypoxic drive". A target of 88–92% cuts mortality compared with high-flow oxygen; STG gives PaO2 8 kPa or SpO2 90% and allows a lower target when ventilation is scarce and the patient is improving [STG]. Pulse oximetry over-reads in darker skin [GOLD], so confirm with a blood gas.

Ventilation (BTS/ICS 2016; ERS/ATS 2017)

  • NIV succeeds in 80–85%, and reduces intubation and mortality. About 20% of acidotic patients correct with medical treatment and controlled oxygen alone, which is why BTS waits an hour unless the patient is tiring.
  • HFNT (high-flow nasal oxygen): GOLD 2026 now lists it first for hypoxaemic failure, but ERS guidance prefers NIV for hypercapnic failure; about 30% switch from HFNT to NIV. At George, HFNT is for those who cannot tolerate NIV DECISION.
  • Intubation: failure of NIV, arrest, GCS < 8, pH < 7.15 or worsening, unable to clear secretions. In-hospital mortality after IMV is 17–49%, but doctors underestimate survival; patients with a reversible trigger, good mobility and no home oxygen do well.
  • IMV settings: low rate (10–15), I:E 1:3 or longer, tidal volume 6–8 mL/kg, accept pH 7.20–7.25; aim for the patient's usual PaCO2, not a normal one.

Mitigation
  • No NIV: optimise bronchodilators, keep SpO2 88–92%, treat the cause, consultant review; refer early.
  • No ABG: VBG for pH and PCO2; capillary (earlobe) gas where available.

Drug treatment: the evidence

  • Bronchodilators: MDI with spacer equals a nebuliser for FEV1; nebulisers are easier when very breathless. Continue long-acting inhalers through the admission if the patient can use them [GOLD].
  • Corticosteroid: shortens recovery and stay, lowers relapse. Five days is enough; longer courses add pneumonia and death. Eosinophil-guided steroids (low eosinophils, no steroid) are proven only in primary care so far.
  • Antibiotic: clear benefit in ICU patients; purulent sputum is 94% sensitive for a high bacterial load. CRP-guided prescribing cuts antibiotic use safely: SAASP withholds antibiotics at CRP < 40 mg/L when only one cardinal symptom is present (amoxicillin or doxycycline if ≥ 40) [SAASP 2026]. Procalcitonin: GOLD does not recommend it; SAASP says a low value allows antibiotics to be withheld. Ambulatory patients: conditional recommendation for antibiotics [ERS/ATS].
  • Not useful: aminophylline, respiratory stimulants, routine chest physiotherapy (only for those who cannot clear secretions) [SATS].

Before discharge (GOLD Fig 4.10)

  • Maintenance inhaler started before discharge, technique checked, and a written plan for the next exacerbation.
  • Smoking: ask, advise, refer; home oxygen is refused to active smokers (GRH LTDOT SOP).
  • Vaccines: influenza yearly, pneumococcal, COVID-19; RSV over 60 if available (GRH 2024).
  • Home oxygen: assess when stable at follow-up (PaO2 < 7.3 kPa, not smoking for 3 months) [STG]; blood gases at discharge overestimate the need.
  • Follow-up within 4 weeks, again at 12–16 weeks with spirometry. CT for bronchiectasis if exacerbations recur.
After a ventilated episode
  • Recurrence is likely: discuss future NIV and intubation with the patient before discharge [BTS/ICS]
  • Persistent hypercapnia (PaCO2 > 7 kPa 2–4 weeks later): consider home NIV referral CHECK source
  • Palliative care for breathlessness when appropriate
