Definition (UpToDate 2025)

Haemoptysis is blood coughed up from below the vocal cords. Patients die from asphyxiation, not blood loss: a small volume can fill the airway of a patient with little lung reserve, and the Hb may be normal. So the useful definition of life-threatening (formerly "massive") haemoptysis is clinical: bleeding that obstructs the airway, impairs gas exchange or causes haemodynamic instability. Shaw (2020) adds a high-risk group to treat cautiously at any volume: post-TB cavities (especially with an aspergilloma), active TB with a possible Rasmussen aneurysm, bleeding beyond 48 hrs despite treatment, pregnancy and anticoagulation.

Where the blood comes from (UpToDate)

  • Bronchial arteries (systemic pressure): only 2% of lung blood flow, but the source in about 90% of life-threatening bleeds. Chronic inflammation (post-TB cavities, bronchiectasis) makes them enlarge and become tortuous, often with collaterals from intercostal, internal mammary and phrenic arteries.
  • Pulmonary arteries (low pressure): about 5%. A Rasmussen aneurysm is a pseudoaneurysm in the wall of a TB cavity; rupture is often fatal. Also arteriovenous malformations and PE with infarction.
  • Aorta and other systemic arteries: about 5% (fistulas, aneurysms).

Causes

Worldwide the commonest causes of life-threatening haemoptysis are bronchiectasis, lung cancer, TB and fungal infection. In the Western Cape TB dominates: of 101 consecutive patients at Tygerberg, 76% had had TB and 64% had active TB; 26% had bronchiectasis and 10% an aspergilloma; only 7% had lung cancer [Gross 2009]. Haemoptysis made up 9.8% of acute respiratory admissions there [Shaw 2023]. Less common: lung abscess, mitral stenosis and LV failure, PE, diffuse alveolar haemorrhage (vasculitis, lupus, anti-GBM disease), coagulopathy and anticoagulants, Kaposi sarcoma, trauma and cocaine.

CT or bronchoscopy?

CT (with contrast; CT angiography if life-threatening) finds bronchiectasis, small cancers, cavities, aspergillomas, aneurysms and AVMs, and maps the arteries for embolisation. Bronchoscopy finds mucosal lesions (bronchitis, Kaposi sarcoma, endobronchial tumour), localises active bleeding and takes samples. Combined, they find 93% of cancers, against 67% for CT and 42% for bronchoscopy alone [UpToDate].

A normal CXR does not exclude cancer: about 10% of smokers with haemoptysis and a normal CXR have one; with heavy bleeding and a normal CXR, 70% have bronchiectasis on CT [UpToDate].

First measures (UpToDate)

  • Positioning bleeding side down keeps blood out of the good lung. Aspiration into the other lung is the strongest predictor of death [Alexander 2013]. The side is often clear from a known cavity; auscultation can mislead because blood pools by gravity.
  • Airway: a large ETT (≥ 8.0) lets the bronchoscopist suction clot. Single-lung intubation protects the good lung: easy into the right main bronchus (left-sided bleeding), harder into the left without a bronchoscope. Double-lumen tubes need expertise and block with clot.
  • Tranexamic acid: weak evidence. In one RCT (47 patients, non-life-threatening), nebulised tranexamic acid 500 mg reduced bleeding volume and hospital stay; toxicity is rare. Systemic tranexamic acid is widely used in SA practice [Shaw 2020] but not tested in a trial for haemoptysis.
  • Cough suppression and bed rest are traditional in every SA protocol (Metro West, Shaw, Paarl) with little trial evidence; the risk is sedating a patient who must clear the airway.

Embolisation and surgery

  • Bronchial artery embolisation (BAE) stops bleeding in 60–92%; 10–20% rebleed in 6–12 months, more with aspergilloma, TB and bronchiectasis [UpToDate]; 18–42% in TB settings [Gross 2009]. Spinal cord ischaemia occurs in < 1% (0.19% in 8500 cases).
  • Who needs surgery after BAE (Tygerberg risk score, one point each): bleeding > 7 days after BAE, blood transfusion needed, aspergilloma, no active TB. Score 0–1: observe, no recurrence or death at 1 year; 2–4: work-up for resection [Gross 2009]. In this cohort, 21 of 82 stopped bleeding on medical treatment alone within 24 hrs and did as well as those embolised.
  • Emergency resection without BAE for radiologically localised disease in fit patients (Durban, 61 patients): 2% rebled after surgery against 75% rebleeding after BAE in the comparison group [Alexander 2013]. Most centres still embolise first; emergency surgery during active bleeding carries up to 40% mortality.
  • Rasmussen aneurysm: CT angiography shows the pulmonary artery lesion; at Tygerberg a transcatheter vascular plug in the feeding artery succeeded technically in 25 of 25, and 72% were bleeding-free at 48 hrs [Shaw 2023].

Prognosis: mortality 7–30%; 24% overall at Tygerberg, where 12 patients died before any procedure could be done [Gross 2009].

Tertiary options

BAE (interventional radiology), pulmonary artery plugging, rigid or flexible bronchoscopy (iced saline, adrenaline, balloon blockade), lung resection. Diffuse alveolar haemorrhage does not respond to embolisation: treat the cause (immunosuppression, plasma exchange). Centre and route CHECK

Mitigation
  • No CT angiography: refer on the CXR and the history; a known cavity or aspergilloma is enough to plan.
  • No ICU bed: high-care bed, suction at the bedside, bleeding side down, intubation plan agreed with anaesthesia.
  • Not a candidate (end-stage post-TB lung, metastatic cancer): ceiling of care written, comfort care.
